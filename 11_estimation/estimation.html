Point Estimation

Now as we know a lot about sample mean distribution, we come back to our main question - how to use it to learn something about population mean.

NotePoint estimator

Let \(\theta\) be a statistic used to guess the value of population parameter \(y\). We say that \(\theta\) is an estimator of \(y\).

Examples

  • \(\bar{x}\) is an estimator of \(\mu\)
  • \(s^2\) is an estimator of \(\sigma^2\)

A good estimator must be a good guess. What is the good guess?

NoteUnbiased estimator

If \(E(\theta)=y\), then \(\theta\) is called unbiased estimator of \(y\)

Examples

  • \(\bar{x}\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\mu\)
  • \(s^2\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2\) (but if you divide by \(n\) instead of \(n-1\) then it becomes biased!)

Example: The German Tank Problem & Estimator Efficiency

To motivate the study of estimation, we look at a famous historical case from WWII. The Allies needed to estimate German tank production (\(a\)) using serial numbers (\(x_i\)) from captured tanks.

Historical Context: Intelligence vs. Statistics

Allied intelligence used traditional methods (spies), while statisticians used serial number analysis. The statistical estimates were significantly more accurate.

June 1941 1,550 244 271
August 1942 1,550 327 342

1. The Simplified Model

Assume serial numbers are distributed uniformly: \(x_i \sim U[0, a]\). We want to estimate the maximum value \(a\).

Case \(n=1\): Suppose we capture one tank, \(x_1\).

  • Since \(E[x_1] = \frac{a}{2}\), the estimator \(\hat{\theta} = x_1\) is biased because \(E[x_1] \neq a\).
  • To fix this, we define \(\hat{\theta}_{single} = 2x_1\).
  • Check for unbiasedness: \(E[2x_1] = 2 E[x_1] = 2(\frac{a}{2}) = a\). It is unbiased.

Case \(n > 1\): If we have many tanks, we can use the sample mean \(\bar{x}\).

  • Define \(\hat{\theta}_{mean} = 2\bar{x}\).
  • \(E[2\bar{x}] = 2 E[\bar{x}] = 2(\frac{a}{2}) = a\). This is also unbiased.

2. Relative Efficiency

We have two unbiased estimators: \(2x_1\) and \(2\bar{x}\). Which is “better”? We define “better” as having a smaller Variance (the “Relative Efficiency” criterion).

Calculating Variances:

  • For the single observation: \[Var(2x_1) = 4 Var(x_1) = 4 \left( \frac{a^2}{12} \right) = \frac{a^2}{3}\]
  • For the sample mean: \[Var(2\bar{x}) = 4 Var(\bar{x}) = 4 \left( \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \right) = 4 \left( \frac{a^2/12}{n} \right) = \frac{a^2}{3n}\]

Conclusion: Since \(Var(2\bar{x}) = \frac{1}{n} Var(2x_1)\), the estimator using the sample mean is \(n\) times more efficient. Using all available data reduces our Expected Squared Error, providing a much more reliable “guess” of enemy production.

TipIntuition: why variance?

Variance measures the “risk” of your guess. Even if both methods are right on average (unbiased), the one with lower variance is much more likely to be close to the true value in any single sample.

NoteEfficiency of an estimator

An unbiased estimator is called more efficient than another if it has a smaller variance.

In our example, the estimator based on the sample mean is more efficient than the one based on a single observation, making it a superior choice for estimating the population parameter \(a\).

The Sample Mean as an Estimator of the Population Mean

While the sample mean \(\bar{x}\) is a “standard” estimator of \(\mu\), it is not always the “best.”

TipComparison
  • Bias: \(\bar{x}\) is always unbiased for the population mean \(\mu\), regardless of whether the population is Normal, Binomial, or Poisson etc.
  • Efficiency: \(\bar{x}\) is not always the most efficient estimator. For example, if the population is Uniform, the estimator based on the maximum observation can be more efficient than \(\bar{x}\). This depends on the assumption about the underlying distribution of the population.

Consistency

ImportantConsistency

An estimator is consistent if it converges to the true population parameter as the sample size \(n\) increases toward infinity.

For this to happen, both the bias and the variance of the estimator must shrink to zero as \(n \to \infty\).

Remarks

  • Biased but Consistent: The estimator may have a systematic error in small samples, but that error disappears as you collect more data. For example, the sample variance estimator that divides by \(n\) instead of \(n-1\) is biased for finite samples, but it becomes consistent as \(n\) grows large.

  • Unbiased but Inconsistent: The estimator is centered on the truth, but its uncertainty never shrinks. Collecting more data doesn’t make the estimate more precise (trivial example is using only the first observation \(X_1\) to estimate \(\mu\)).

The Bottom Line: Consistency is the “large sample minimum.” In modern econometrics, we often tolerate small-sample bias as long as we are guaranteed to reach the truth with enough data.

The Trio of Properties: Bias, Consistency, and Efficiency

NoteThe Ideal Estimator

An “ideal” estimator is Unbiased, Consistent, and Efficient.

Comparison

Property Focus Purpose
Unbiasedness Accuracy Ensuring we are “on target” on average.
Consistency Reliability Ensuring error disappears as \(n \to \infty\).
Efficiency Precision Ensuring we have the smallest possible error among competitors.
ImportantWhy Efficiency Matters

If \(\hat{\theta}_1\) and \(\hat{\theta}_2\) are both unbiased and consistent, we choose the one that is more efficient. Higher efficiency means your confidence intervals will be narrower and your hypothesis tests more powerful.

Interval Estimation

From Point to Interval Estimation

TipMain Question

What can we infer about the population mean \(\mu\) from a simple random sample?

We previously used the sample average \(\bar{x}\) as our single best “guess” for \(\mu\):

  • Our guess is unbiased, meaning that on average, \(\bar{x}\) equals \(\mu\).
  • and consistent, as \(n\) increases, it becomes increasingly precise.

Limitations:

  • A point estimate is a single number, which almost never equals the population mean \(\mu\).
  • No information on the reliability of the guess or the degree of uncertainty.

Both points are adressed by an interval estimate that captures the uncertainty around our guess.

Note

As \(n\) grows, the distribution of sample mean narrows (the standard error shrinks), but we still need a formal way to communicate the “buffer” around our estimate.

Example

Here is an example of a typical graph you can see in research papers. This one is from a paper by Allcott et al. (2020) that estimates the effect of social media on well-being.

The graph shows how deactivating Facebook for one month affected several measures of happiness and well-being.

  • The Zero Line: This is the baseline of the “Control Group” (the students who kept using Facebook as usual).

  • The Dots (Point Estimates): These represent the average change for the “Treatment Group” (the people who quit) . If a dot is to the right of the zero line, it means that group felt better (happier, less anxious, or more satisfied) than the group that stayed on the app.

  • The Bars (Confidence Intervals): The horizontal lines (whiskers) represent our uncertainty. If the whisker does not touch the red line, the result is “statistically significant” (we are 95% sure it’s not a fluke). If the whisker crosses the red line, the result is “insignificant”—meaning we can’t be sure if there was a real change or just random noise in the data.

Interval Estimation of Population Mean when \(\sigma\) is Known

NoteIntuition: Interval Estimate

An interval estimate (or Confidence Interval) is a range of values (interval), derived from sample statistics, that are “reasonable” guesses for an unknown population parameter.

For simplicity, we first assume that the population standard deviation \(\sigma\) is known, and the only uncertainty is about the sample mean \(\bar{x}\). Later we will relax this assumption and discuss the case when \(\sigma\) is unknown.

Begin by recalling that CLT says that the sampling distribution of the sample mean \(\bar{x}\) is approximately Normal: \[\bar{x} \sim N\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\]

Lets explore the logic on a specific example (population is generated by a dice roll):

  • Fixed Sample Mean value: \(\bar{x} = 3\)
  • Population Variance: \(\sigma^2 = 2.92\)
  • Sample Size: \(n = 20\)
  • Standard Error: \(SE = \sqrt{\frac{2.92}{20}} \approx 0.382\)

Question: how likely is it to observe \(\bar{x} = 3\) or a more extreme value if the true population mean \(\mu\) were equal to the value in the slider?

“More extreme” means that we are looking at the tails of the distribution that are as far or farther from \(\mu\) than our observed \(\bar{x}\).

NoteDefinition: P-Value Intuition

The shaded regions represent the probability of obtaining a sample mean at least as “extreme” as our observed value (\(\bar{x} = 3\)), assuming the slider’s value is the true population mean \(\mu\).

ImportantKey Result

As the hypothesized \(\mu\) moves further away from the observed \(\bar{x}\), the shaded “tail” area (the p-value) shrinks. Observing such value of \(\bar{x}\) becomes less likely, which is why values of \(\mu\) far from \(\bar{x}\) are considered less plausible.

So, given our fixed value for the sample mean \(\bar{x}\), we can find a range of \(\mu\) values for which the p-value is sufficiently large (greater than \(\alpha\)), meaning that those values of \(\mu\) are plausible given our observed data.

Mathematical Derivation of Confidence Interval

Lets find an interval around \(\mu\) that contains \(\bar{x}\) with probability \(1-\alpha\): \[P(\mu - \text{margin} \le \bar{x} \le \mu + \text{margin}) = 1-\alpha\]

Here \(\alpha\) is the probability of the interval not containing \(\mu\), usually referred to as the significance level.

The exact values of the margins we can find by standardizing the sample mean \(\bar{x}\) to the standard normal distribution: \[P(-\text{margin} \le \bar{x} - \mu \le \text{margin}) = 1-\alpha\]

further dividing by the standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), we get: \[P\left(-\frac{\text{margin}}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \le Z \le \frac{\text{margin}}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\right) = 1-\alpha.\]

We define the critical value \(z_{\alpha/2}\) such that it satisfied: \[P\left(-z_{\alpha/2} \le \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \le z_{\alpha/2}\right) = 1-\alpha.\]

From here, we can see that our margins are

\[\text{margin} = z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.\]

Summarizing the steps: \[P(\mu - z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \le \bar{x} \le \mu + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}) = 1-\alpha\]

The above expression is equivalent to: \[P(\bar{x} - z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \le \mu \le \bar{x} + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}) = 1-\alpha.\]

Important

The probability that a random interval \(\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) contains the true population mean \(\mu\) is \(1-\alpha\). In other words, if we were to repeat the sampling process many times and construct a confidence interval from each sample, we would expect approximately \((1-\alpha)\) fraction of those intervals to contain \(\mu\).

Hence, the above interval contains a useful information on top of a simple point estimate \(\bar{x}\): it quantifies the uncertainty around our estimate by providing a range of plausible values for \(\mu\) based on the variability in the data and the sample size.

TipConfidence Interval for \(\mu\) when \(\sigma\) is Known

Given a population with known variance \(\sigma^2\) and a sample of size \(n\), the confidence interval for \(\mu\) is:

\[\text{CI}_{1-\alpha} = \left[ \bar{x} - z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}, \bar{x} + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right]\]

Where the components are defined as:

  • Critical Value (\(z_{\alpha/2}\)): The value from the standard normal distribution \(N(0,1)\) such that the area in each tail is \(\alpha/2\).
  • Standard Error (\(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)): The standard deviation of the the sample mean, given known population standard deviation \(\sigma\) and sample size \(n\).
  • Half the width of the confidence interval, representing the margin of error, is \(z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.\) Hence, \(z_{\alpha/2}\) is the number of standard errors we need to go out from the sample mean \(\bar{x}\) to capture the true mean \(\mu\) with confidence level \(1-\alpha\).

Numerical Example from Class Data

Data on girls’ heights in the class:

  • Sample size \(n=185\)
  • Sample mean \(\bar{x} = 165.9\) cm
  • Population standard deviation assumed to be known: \(\sigma = 8.7\) cm
  • Estimate a 90% confidence interval for the true mean height.

Computations

Constructing a 90% confidence interval for \(\mu\) given: \(n = 185\), \(\bar{x} = 165.9\), and \(\sigma = 8.7\).

Step 1: Find the Critical Value

For a 90% confidence level, \(\alpha = 0.10\) and \(\alpha/2 = 0.05\). \[z_{0.05} \approx 1.645\]

Step 2: Calculate the Standard Error

\[SE = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{8.7}{\sqrt{185}} \approx 0.640\]

Step 3: Calculate the Margin of Error

\[ME = z_{\alpha/2} \times SE = 1.645 \times 0.640 \approx 1.053\]

Step 4: Construct the Interval

\[CI_{0.90} = [165.9 \pm 1.053] = [164.847, 166.953]\]

Interpretation: if we were to take many samples of size 185 from the population of girls and compute a confidence interval from each sample, we would expect approximately 95% of those intervals to contain the true mean height \(\mu\).

NoteIntuition: Plausibility of Values

A value like \(164\) cm is outside our interval. If the true population mean were actually \(164\) cm, the probability of observing a sample mean as high as \(165.9\) would be less than 10%. Because this result is “unlikely” under that hypothesis, we exclude \(164\) cm from our range of plausible values.

Common Z-Scores

Confidence Level Alpha (\(\alpha\)) Tail Area (\(\alpha/2\)) Crit. Value (\(z_{\alpha/2}\))
90% 0.10 0.05 1.645
95% 0.05 0.025 1.960
99% 0.01 0.005 2.576
NoteDefinition: Critical Value (\(z_{\alpha/2}\))

The critical value is the z-score that bounds the upper \(\alpha/2\) proportion of the standard normal distribution.

ImportantKey Result: The Precision-Confidence Trade-off

Increasing the confidence level (e.g., from 95% to 99%) requires a larger critical value. This results in a wider, less precise confidence interval. To maintain precision at higher confidence levels, the sample size \(n\) must be increased.

Tip95% Confidence Interval for Heights

\[CI_{0.95} = \left[ 165.9 - 1.96 \times \frac{8.7}{\sqrt{185}}, 165.9 + 1.96 \times \frac{8.7}{\sqrt{185}} \right] = [163.5, 168.3].\]

Tip99% Confidence Interval for Heights

\[CI_{0.99} = \left[ 165.9 - 2.576 \times \frac{8.7}{\sqrt{185}}, 165.9 + 2.576 \times \frac{8.7}{\sqrt{185}} \right] = [162.4, 169.4].\]

The Informativeness of CIs

A confidence interval is most informative when it is narrow, indicating high precision. The width is determined by the Margin of Error (ME):

\[ME = z_{\alpha/2} \left( \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right)\]

Drivers of Interval Width

Factor Change Effect on CI Width Precision
Sample Size (\(n\)) Increase (\(\uparrow\)) Narrows Increases
Confidence Level Increase (\(\uparrow\)) Widens Decreases
Population SD (\(\sigma\)) Increase (\(\uparrow\)) Widens Decreases
NoteDefinition: Precision vs. Confidence

Precision refers to the narrowness of the interval. Confidence refers to our certainty that the interval contains the true \(\mu\). There is an inherent trade-off: to be more confident without increasing \(n\), you must accept a less precise (wider) interval.

ImportantKey Result: The “Power” of \(n\)

Because \(n\) is in the denominator of the Standard Error, increasing the sample size is the only way for a researcher to increase both precision and confidence. Note that you must quadruple \(n\) to cut the interval width in half.
