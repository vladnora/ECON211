Drafting Lecture 2

Interval Estimation

From Point to Interval Estimation

TipMain Question

What can we infer about the population mean \(\mu\) from a simple random sample?

We previously used the sample average \(\bar{x}\) as our single best “guess” for \(\mu\):

  • Our guess is unbiased, meaning that on average, \(\bar{x}\) equals \(\mu\).
  • and consistent, as \(n\) increases, it becomes increasingly precise.

The limitations of this approach are that:

  • A point estimate is just a single number, which almost never equals the true population mean \(\mu\).
  • It provides no information on the reliability of the guess or the degree of uncertainty.

The Next Step is to move from a single point estimate to an interval estimate that captures the uncertainty around our guess.

Note

As \(n\) grows, the distribution of sample mean narrows (the standard error shrinks), but we still need a formal way to communicate the “buffer” around our estimate.

Interval Estimation of Population Mean when \(\sigma\) is Known

NoteIntuition: Interval Estimate

An interval estimate (or Confidence Interval) is a range of values (interval), derived from sample statistics, that are “reasonable” guesses for an unknown population parameter.

For simplicity, we first assume that the population standard deviation \(\sigma\) is known, and the only uncertainty is about the sample mean \(\bar{x}\). Later we will relax this assumption and discuss the case when \(\sigma\) is unknown.

Begin by recalling that CLT says that the sampling distribution of the sample mean \(\bar{x}\) is approximately Normal: \[\bar{x} \sim N\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\]

Lets find an interval around \(\mu\) that contains \(\bar{x}\) with probability \(1-\alpha\): \[P(\mu - \text{margin} \le \bar{x} \le \mu + \text{margin}) = 1-\alpha\]

Here \(\alpha\) is the probability of the interval not containing \(\mu\), usually referred to as the significance level.

The exact values of the margins we can find by standardizing the sample mean \(\bar{x}\) to the standard normal distribution: \[P(-\text{margin} \le \bar{x} - \mu \le \text{margin}) = 1-\alpha\]

further dividing by the standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), we get: \[P\left(-\frac{\text{margin}}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \le Z \le \frac{\text{margin}}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\right) = 1-\alpha.\]

We define the critical value \(z_{\alpha/2}\) such that it satisfied: \[P\left(-z_{\alpha/2} \le \frac{\bar{x} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \le z_{\alpha/2}\right) = 1-\alpha.\]

From here, we can see that our margins are

\[\text{margin} = z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.\]

Summarizing the steps: \[P(\mu - z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \le \bar{x} \le \mu + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}) = 1-\alpha\]

The above expression is equivalent to: \[P(\bar{x} - z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \le \mu \le \bar{x} + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}) = 1-\alpha.\]

Important

The probability that a random interval \(\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) contains the true population mean \(\mu\) is \(1-\alpha\). In other words, if we were to repeat the sampling process many times and construct a confidence interval from each sample, we would expect approximately \((1-\alpha)\) fraction of those intervals to contain \(\mu\).

Hence, the above interval contains a useful information on top of a simple point estimate \(\bar{x}\): it quantifies the uncertainty around our estimate by providing a range of plausible values for \(\mu\) based on the variability in the data and the sample size.

TipConfidence Interval for \(\mu\) when \(\sigma\) is Known

Given a population with known variance \(\sigma^2\) and a sample of size \(n\), the confidence interval for \(\mu\) is:

\[\text{CI}_{1-\alpha} = \left[ \bar{x} - z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}, \bar{x} + z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right]\]

Where the components are defined as:

  • Critical Value (\(z_{\alpha/2}\)): The value from the standard normal distribution \(N(0,1)\) such that the area in each tail is \(\alpha/2\).
  • Standard Error (\(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)): The standard deviation of the the sample mean, given known population standard deviation \(\sigma\) and sample size \(n\).
  • Half the width of the confidence interval, representing the margin of error, is \(z_{\alpha/2} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\). Hence, \(z_{\alpha/2}\) is the number of standard errors we need to go out from the sample mean \(\bar{x}\) to capture the true mean \(\mu\) with confidence level \(1-\alpha\).

Let’s explore why the confidence interval can be thought of a range of plausible or reasonable values for \(\mu\).

Suppose we have a sample mean \(\bar{x} = 5\), a known population standard deviation \(\sigma = 2\), and a sample size \(n = 100\). We want to construct a 95% confidence interval for the population mean \(\mu\). 1. First, we find the critical value \(z_{\alpha/2}\) for a 95% confidence level. Since \(\alpha = 0.05\), we have \(\alpha/2 = 0.025\). The critical value corresponding to this is approximately \(z_{0.025} = 1.96\). 2. Next, we calculate the standard error: \[\text{SE} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{100}} = \frac{2}{10} = 0.2.\] 3. Now we can calculate the margin of error: \[\text{Margin of Error} = z_{\alpha/2} \times \text{SE} = 1.96 \times 0.2 = 0.392.\] 4. Finally, we construct the confidence interval: \[\text{CI}_{0.95} = \left[ \bar{x} - \text{Margin of Error}, \bar{x} + \text{Margin of Error} \right] = \left[ 5 - 0.392, 5 + 0.392 \right] = [4.608, 5.392].\]

Remember, that the true population mean \(\mu\) is fixed, but we don’t know its value. The key to understanding why values inside the interval are more plausible than those outside is to ask how likely we would have observed a sample mean of \(\bar{x} = 5\) if the true mean were, say, \(\mu = 4.5\) (which is outside the interval) versus if the true mean were \(\mu = 5\) (which is inside the interval).

To answer this, lets imagine real \(\mu\) is 4.5. The sampling distribution of \(\bar{x}\) would be centered around 4.5, and the probability of observing a sample mean as extreme as 5 (or more extreme) would be relatively low, since 5 is quite far from 4.5 in terms of standard errors.
